An AI prototype answers a narrow question: *Can a model learn something useful from the available data?* A factory deployment answers a wider one: *Can a complete system deliver a trustworthy output to the right operational decision, under real constraints, day after day?*

The gap is not just better model accuracy. It is the work of connecting industrial data, software interfaces, infrastructure, release controls, operators, and failure response. This article is a **general engineering framework**, not a report of a system I have deployed or an experiment I have completed. The [systems overview](/systems) describes the kinds of operational outputs such systems may support; the [architecture library](/architecture) explores their technical boundaries in more detail.

## What a production AI system actually means

A production AI system is a maintained chain that turns source data into a bounded prediction, recommendation, alert, or action within an operational workflow. Its output has a consumer, a required delivery time, a definition of acceptable quality, and a behavior when the system cannot meet that requirement.

The model is one component. A useful production boundary also includes data acquisition and validation, transformations, inference, output delivery, version control, monitoring, security, and human ownership. A score that exists only in a notebook is not yet an operational decision.

<Callout title="General engineering principle" tone="note">
Define the decision before the model: who will act on the output, what they can do, how quickly they need it, and what happens if the output is missing or wrong.
</Callout>

## Why prototypes fail during deployment

Prototypes often work with static extracts, manually cleaned records, known labels, and a single execution environment. Factory systems encounter changing recipes, equipment states, maintenance events, delayed signals, incomplete context, software updates, and ownership boundaries. A validation metric from an offline split does not test these conditions by itself.

Common gaps are easier to discuss as failure modes than as a vague demand to “productionize” a model.

## Industrial data requirements and ingestion

Industrial data is more than a sensor value. A measurement becomes interpretable when it has source identity, timestamp quality, units, asset context, operating state, and a known relationship to the decision. For example, a temperature value may mean different things during startup, steady operation, cleaning, or shutdown. The design must distinguish these regimes before treating their records as interchangeable.

Data ingestion should preserve raw observations and source timestamps, even when downstream processing creates a standardized representation. Specify expected frequency, permitted delay, missing-value policy, duplication behavior, schema evolution, and replay requirements. If a source is disconnected, decide whether to buffer, skip inference, or continue with a bounded local state. Never silently treat stale data as current.

This is also a governance problem. Determine who owns each source, who can change its schema, how access is granted, and how downstream consumers are notified. The [manufacturing data pipeline architecture](/architecture/manufacturing-data-pipeline) describes a conceptual acquisition-to-data-product flow.

## Processing data for a real decision

Processing turns acquired signals into decision-ready inputs. It may align timestamps, convert units, validate ranges, join equipment and production context, form event-time windows, or derive features. Each operation should have a documented rationale and a version. The aim is not to make data look tidy; it is to retain the information required for a defensible decision.

Two errors deserve special attention. **Leakage** occurs when training inputs contain information unavailable at inference time. **Training-serving skew** occurs when offline and online transformations differ. Prevent both by defining the inference-time information boundary first, then testing the same feature logic against historical and live-style records. Keep rejected records visible as quality evidence rather than dropping them without trace.

## Model development and evaluation

Model development begins with an explicit target and baseline. Is the system predicting a future condition, ranking options, flagging a deviation, or supporting a workflow? What simple rule or existing process should a candidate model outperform? How are costs of missed events, false alerts, delayed decisions, and operator burden represented?

Use evaluation splits that respect time, assets, and operating regimes where those boundaries matter. Report performance across relevant conditions, not only one aggregate number. Check calibration when the output is a probability; check threshold behavior when alerts are routed to people; check constraints when an optimization output may affect equipment or scheduling. A technically impressive model can still be unsuitable if its useful warning window is too short or its output cannot be acted on.

Maintain reproducibility: identify the training data snapshot, label definition, transformation version, code, configuration, evaluation criteria, and artifact. That provenance is what allows a release to be compared, rolled back, or investigated later.

## Inference and deployment

Inference is a service obligation, not merely a call to `predict()`. Define input and output schemas, request identity, latency budget, throughput, timeout, idempotency, and behavior on low-quality input. If a model cannot give a trustworthy answer for a record, a designed “no decision” state is often safer than a plausible-looking score.

Deployment should promote an immutable model artifact with compatible configuration and transformations. Before release, test the interface, resource needs, security permissions, and representative operating conditions. A staged rollout or shadow mode can compare behavior without making the new output authoritative. Rollback must include both model and alert or decision policy if those are versioned separately.

### Choosing edge, cloud, or hybrid inference

Edge inference can keep a time-sensitive path near equipment and reduce dependency on wide-area connectivity. Cloud inference can centralize compute, governance, and updates. Neither is intrinsically “production-ready.” Placement follows the operational budget: required response time, data volume and movement, connectivity, hardware limits, plant security policy, and fleet maintenance effort.

For a hybrid design, label which path is authoritative when connectivity is lost. If inference remains local, the edge runtime needs a controlled artifact, health checks, bounded storage, and a recoverable update process. If inference depends on cloud availability, the consuming workflow needs a fallback. The [edge AI architecture note](/architecture/edge-ai-architecture) examines this boundary.

## Monitoring, drift, and observability

Monitoring must cover more than endpoint uptime. A useful set of layers is: source freshness and quality; processing lag and rejects; inference availability and latency; model output distributions; delivery to the operational consumer; and outcome feedback where measurable. Every signal should carry enough version and asset context to support investigation.

**Data drift** is a change in input distribution or operating context relative to a reference period. It may be harmless, expected, or serious. **Model drift** is a change in model behavior or usefulness for the target decision. In practice, both need context: a new production recipe can change sensor distributions without making a model unusable, while an unchanged aggregate distribution can hide a problem in one asset or regime. Drift detection is an investigation trigger, not automatic proof that retraining will help.

Observability connects symptoms across layers. If alert volume rises, can the team tell whether source data changed, a transformation version changed, a threshold changed, or the process itself changed? Dashboards and alerts should have owners, expected ranges, escalation rules, and runbooks. The [AI observability architecture](/architecture/ai-observability-architecture) gives a conceptual layered model.

## Reliability, security, and operational integration

Reliability means the system behaves predictably when it cannot behave ideally. Define fallback, abstention, local buffering, retries, backpressure, timeout, and recovery for the decision path. Test failures deliberately: stale source data, disconnected network, unavailable model service, incompatible artifact, and delayed output. Record what happened and whether an operator needs to intervene.

Security requires an explicit boundary between industrial sources, application services, model artifacts, and enterprise or cloud systems. Use least-privilege access, authenticated interfaces, protected secrets, controlled software delivery, and auditable changes. Avoid moving raw operational data farther than the use case requires. Security controls should be designed with latency, availability, and plant maintenance realities in mind—not appended after the deployment diagram is finished.

Operational integration is where the system becomes useful or fails quietly. Specify who sees the output, what context accompanies it, what action is allowed, who can override it, and how feedback is captured. An anomaly score may need an investigation queue rather than an automatic shutdown. A prediction may need its time horizon and uncertainty. An optimization recommendation may need constraints and assumptions displayed. The appropriate interface depends on the decision and its consequence.

## Production deployment checklist

Use this as a design review, not a claim that every system needs the same stack. The answers should be written down and tested against the use case.

1. **Decision:** Is the operational question, output consumer, action, consequence, and fallback defined?
2. **Data:** Are source identity, timestamps, units, context, ownership, quality rules, and retention known?
3. **Ingestion:** Are delays, duplicates, disconnection, replay, schema changes, and stale-input behavior tested?
4. **Processing:** Are transformations versioned, leakage checked, and offline/online parity verified?
5. **Model:** Is there a baseline and evaluation across relevant time, assets, regimes, and decision costs?
6. **Inference:** Are schemas, latency, capacity, abstention, errors, and output delivery measurable?
7. **Deployment:** Are artifact provenance, compatibility, staged release, rollback, and approval clear?
8. **Placement:** Is edge/cloud placement justified by latency, connectivity, data movement, and operations?
9. **Monitoring:** Are data, service, model, workflow, and outcome signals correlated by version and context?
10. **Drift:** Are changes reviewed against operational context before retraining or release decisions?
11. **Reliability:** Have likely failures and safe degraded behavior been exercised?
12. **Security:** Are trust boundaries, identity, least privilege, secrets, and change audit documented?
13. **Operations:** Is there a named owner, runbook, escalation path, and operator feedback mechanism?

## The real transition is from model to system

A prototype can justify further investigation. It cannot, by itself, establish that the complete operating chain is reliable. The transition to production begins when data contracts, decision boundaries, deployment policy, monitoring, security, and human ownership become first-class design work.
